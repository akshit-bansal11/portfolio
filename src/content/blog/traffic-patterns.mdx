## A locked front door and open offices

Picture an office building with excellent front-door security. There's a guard, a badge reader and a visitor log. But once you're past the lobby, every office door is unlocked, and nobody asks who you are as you walk from floor to floor.

In late 2013, attackers got into the US retailer Target's network using login credentials stolen from a refrigeration and HVAC contractor that had access for its work. That first foothold wasn't anywhere near the payment systems. From there, the attackers moved through the internal network until they reached the checkout terminals in Target's stores. Data from about 40 million credit and debit cards was stolen. A US Senate committee later criticised Target for giving network access to a vendor whose security practices didn't meet common standards.

The lesson people took from breaches like this one: watching who comes in through the front door isn't enough. You also have to care about who talks to whom **inside** the building.

<MarginNote label="two directions">
  in through the front door = north-south. walking between offices = east-west.
</MarginNote>

Engineers describe these two kinds of movement with compass directions: **north-south** traffic and **east-west** traffic. This post covers what they mean, why east-west traffic exploded in the last fifteen years, the tools for each (load balancers, API gateways, service meshes and sidecars) and why "zero trust" between services became the norm. Along the way we'll look at how Lyft, Netflix, Google, Target and Razorpay handled it. There's a demo where you can let an intruder in and watch what they can reach.

## What are north-south and east-west traffic?

When engineers draw a data centre, they usually put the internet at the top of the page and the servers below. So:

- **North-south traffic** crosses the boundary of your system. A user's phone asks your app for their feed (southbound), and the answer goes back out (northbound). Traffic between your data centre and the outside world (users, partner APIs, payment networks) is north-south.
- **East-west traffic** stays inside. Your web service asks the orders service for a user's orders, which asks the payments service for a payment status, which reads the payments database. All of that is east-west: servers talking to servers, sideways across the page.

</Figure>

A few more terms we'll need:

- A **load balancer** spreads incoming requests across several copies of a service, so no single server is overwhelmed. Some work at the network level (**Layer 4**: they only see addresses and ports), others understand HTTP (**Layer 7**: they can route based on the URL or headers).
- An **API gateway** is a smarter front door. It checks who you are (authentication), applies rate limits, and routes each request to the right internal service.
- A **sidecar** is a small helper program that runs right next to each service, like a sidecar attached to a motorbike, and handles the service's network traffic for it.
- A **service mesh** is what you get when every service has a sidecar, plus a central brain that configures them all.

## Where this came from

In the early web era, most applications were built in three layers: a web server, an application server and a database, often called a **three-tier architecture**. Most of the interesting traffic was north-south: a request came in, went down through the layers, and the answer went back out. Networks were built to match. The classic data centre network was a tree: servers plugged into **access** switches, which fed **aggregation** switches, which fed a big **core** switch at the top that connected to the outside world.

Then three things changed.

First, big internet companies started splitting work across many machines. A single search or feed request might fan out to hundreds of servers, each holding a slice of the data, and their answers would be combined. Second, **virtualisation** and later **containers** packed many small workloads onto shared hardware, with workloads moving around constantly. Third, **microservices** split applications into dozens or hundreds of small services that call each other over the network. One click from a user can now trigger a whole chain of internal calls.

The result: most traffic stopped crossing the edge at all. Cisco's Global Cloud Index forecast that more than 70% of data-centre traffic would stay *inside* the data centre. The old tree networks struggled with this, because two servers in different branches had to talk all the way up through the core and back down. So data centres moved to **leaf-spine** networks, where every "leaf" switch connects to every "spine" switch, making any two servers the same short distance apart.

<Figure caption="The same two servers talking. In a three-tier tree, the conversation climbs to a single core and back down. In a leaf-spine network, every path is equally short, and there are many of them to share the load.">

</Figure>

<MarginNote>
  the network literally changed shape because traffic changed direction.
</MarginNote>

Security had to change too. The old model was **perimeter security**, sometimes called castle-and-moat: a strong wall at the edge (firewalls, VPNs) and a trusted network inside. Anything inside the wall was assumed to be friendly. After Google was hit by a sophisticated attack in 2009 (Operation Aurora), it began a project called **BeyondCorp**, described publicly in 2014. Its idea: stop trusting the network. Access should depend on *who* the user is and *what device* they're on, not on whether they happen to be on the office network. That idea, now called **zero trust**, soon spread to service-to-service traffic too.

## How traffic is handled in each direction

### North-south: the front door

A typical request from a user's phone passes through a few layers before it reaches your code:

1. **DNS** turns `api.example.com` into an address.
2. A **CDN** or edge network (a set of servers spread around the world) may answer directly from a cache, or absorb attacks.
3. A **load balancer** picks a healthy server.
4. An **API gateway** checks authentication, applies rate limits, and routes the request to the right internal service.

North-south traffic is the part you *don't* control. It comes from strangers, over the public internet, sometimes with bad intentions. So the front door is where you put the heavy checks: TLS encryption, authentication, rate limiting and protection against attacks.

### East-west: the corridors

East-west traffic has different problems. It's internal, so in the past teams assumed it was safe, but there's *a lot* of it, and every call can fail. Every service that calls another needs answers to the same questions:

- **Discovery:** where are the healthy copies of the payments service right now?
- **Load balancing:** which copy should I call?
- **Resilience:** what timeout? How many retries? (See the blast radius post for why this matters.)
- **Security:** is this really the payments service? Is the orders service *allowed* to call it?
- **Observability:** how long did the call take, and where in the chain did it fail?

The first generation of answers lived **inside each service**, as libraries. Netflix famously built a set of Java libraries for discovery, load balancing and resilience. That works well if every service is written in the same language. But when your services are written in Go, Python, Java and Node, you need to rebuild and maintain those libraries in every language, and keep them all in sync.

### The sidecar: take the network out of the app

The **sidecar** idea moves all of that into a separate proxy process that runs next to every service. The service makes a plain, simple call to `localhost`. Its sidecar handles discovery, load balancing, retries, timeouts, encryption and metrics, then forwards the call to the other service's sidecar, which hands it to the destination.

Lyft built a proxy called **Envoy** for exactly this and open-sourced it in 2016 (case file #1). Envoy became the building block of most service meshes. In 2017, Google, IBM and Lyft announced **Istio**, a **control plane** for Envoy proxies: the central brain that tells every sidecar the routing rules, security policies and certificates to use.

### Zero trust between services

With a sidecar on every service, you can encrypt and authenticate every east-west call using **mutual TLS (mTLS)**. In normal HTTPS, only the server proves who it is. In mTLS, *both sides* show a certificate, so the payments service knows the caller really is the orders service, and vice versa. On top of that identity, you write rules:

```yaml
# Only the payments service may talk to the payments database.
apiVersion: security.istio.io/v1
kind: AuthorizationPolicy
metadata:
  name: payments-db-access
spec:
  selector:
    matchLabels: { app: payments-db }
  rules:
    - from:
        - source: { principals: ["cluster.local/ns/prod/sa/payments"] }
```

Now the corridors have locks, and every lock checks the badge. If an attacker compromises the recommendations service, they can't just walk over to the payments database. The proxy in front of it refuses any caller that isn't Payments.

## Other ways to manage east-west traffic

### Perimeter only

<Analogy title="A castle with a moat">
  Build huge walls and a moat. Inside the castle, everyone wanders freely and trusts each other completely.
</Analogy>

Protect the edge with firewalls and VPNs, and treat the internal network as trusted. Simple, cheap, and how most companies ran for decades.

### Smart libraries in every service

<Analogy title="Every employee carries their own map and keys">
  Instead of hiring staff for every back door, you train every employee to navigate the building, check badges and handle problems themselves.
</Analogy>

Put discovery, retries, timeouts and security into a shared library that every service imports. No extra processes, no extra network hop.

### Send internal traffic through a central gateway

<Analogy title="Every memo goes through the front desk">
  Any time one office wants to send another a note, it walks it down to reception, which logs it and walks it back up.
</Analogy>

Route service-to-service calls through the same kind of central gateway you use for north-south traffic, and enforce rules there.

### Network segmentation by address

<Analogy title="Colour-coded floors">
  Your keycard only opens the floors you're allowed on. Within a floor, everything's open.
</Analogy>

Use firewall rules and separate network segments (VLANs, security groups) so that only certain IP address ranges can reach certain others.

### Sidecar-less meshes

<Analogy title="One concierge per floor instead of one per office">
  Instead of a staff member at every office door, each floor gets a shared concierge who handles everyone's visitors.
</Analogy>

Newer designs move the proxy out of each service and into a shared per-machine component (or into the operating system's kernel, using a technology called eBPF). Istio's **ambient mode**, announced in 2022, works like this.

## Why we usually pick the mesh (and when we don't)

**Perimeter-only security fails the first time the wall is breached.** And with vendors, laptops, CI systems and cloud consoles, the wall gets breached eventually. Target's attackers came in through a contractor's account. Once inside a flat, trusted network, nothing stopped them from moving sideways. Zero trust assumes the breach and limits what it can reach.

**Libraries don't scale across languages and teams.** Every language needs its own version, every bug fix needs to be shipped by every team, and a service that forgets to upgrade becomes the weak link. Lyft's motivation for Envoy was exactly this: an out-of-process proxy works the same way for any language.

**A central gateway for internal traffic becomes a bottleneck and a single point of failure.** Every internal call takes an extra trip through one shared box, adding latency, and if that box has a bad day, *everything* has a bad day. Gateways are great at the edge, where traffic genuinely crosses a boundary.

**IP-based segmentation struggles in a world of containers.** Addresses change every time a container restarts or moves. Rules based on "which IP can talk to which IP" become impossible to keep correct. Identity-based rules ("the payments service can talk to the payments database") don't care where things run.

<MarginNote tone="blue">
  meshes aren't free: every call gets an extra hop through two proxies, and every service gets a roommate eating CPU and memory.
</MarginNote>

**But a mesh has real costs.** Each call passes through two extra proxies, adding a little latency. Each sidecar uses CPU and memory, which adds up across thousands of services. And a mesh is a complex system to run: if the control plane misbehaves, it can affect everything. That's exactly why sidecar-less designs like Istio's ambient mode appeared.

### When the "alternative" is the better choice

For a handful of services in one language, a good library (or just sensible timeouts and TLS) is plenty. Netflix ran one of the world's largest microservice systems for years on in-process Java libraries rather than sidecars, because most of its services were written in Java. When it later adopted Envoy, it published how it made the move with almost no per-service configuration (case file #2). The right tool depended on its language mix and scale at the time.

And for north-south traffic, a gateway is still the right answer, as Razorpay's story shows (case file #5).

## TL;DR

<TLDR>
  <Takeaway>North-south = crossing your edge. East-west = services talking to each other inside.</Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>Microservices made east-west traffic the majority, and changed both network design and security.</Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>Gateways guard the front door. Meshes (a sidecar per service + a control plane) guard the corridors.</Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>Zero trust: check identity on every call, not where it came from. Assume someone is already inside.</Takeaway>
  <Takeaway>Meshes cost latency, memory and complexity. A few services in one language may not need one.</Takeaway>
</TLDR>
