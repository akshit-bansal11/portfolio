## In one minute

- **North-south** traffic crosses your edge: users and partners talking to your servers. **East-west** traffic stays inside: your services calling each other.
- Microservices, containers and big fan-out requests made east-west the bulk of data-centre traffic. Networks and security both had to change shape.
- The front door gets a **load balancer** and an **API gateway**. The corridors get a **service mesh**: a **sidecar** proxy next to every service, plus a **control plane** that configures them all.
- **Zero trust** between services: every call proves who it comes from and is checked against rules, so one stolen login can't walk to the payments database. The price is extra latency, memory and moving parts.

## A locked front door and open offices

Picture an office building with great front-door security: a guard, a badge reader, a visitor log. Past the lobby, every office door is unlocked, and nobody asks who you are as you walk from floor to floor.

- In late 2013 this happened to the US retailer **Target** (case file #4). Attackers logged in with credentials stolen from a refrigeration and HVAC contractor.
- That first foothold was nowhere near the payment systems. They moved sideways through the internal network to the checkout terminals in Target's stores.
- Data from about 40 million credit and debit cards was stolen.

<Callout kind="important">

Watching who comes in through the front door isn't enough. You also have to care about who talks to whom **inside** the building.

</Callout>

## North-south vs east-west

Engineers draw the internet at the top of the page and the servers below it. That gives the two directions their names.

- **North-south** crosses the wall. A phone asks your app for a feed (southbound); the answer goes back out (northbound).
- **East-west** runs sideways, inside. Web asks Orders for a user's orders; Orders asks Payments for a payment status; Payments reads the payments database.

## Why east-west traffic exploded

In the early web, most apps had three layers: a web server, an application server and a database. This is a **three-tier architecture**. A request came in, went down the layers and the answer went back out: mostly north-south. Then three things changed.

<FeatureGrid
  items={[
    { title: "Fan-out", tone: "violet", icon: <Waypoints />, text: "Big internet companies split work across many machines. One search or feed request might fan out to hundreds of servers, each holding a slice of the data, and their answers get combined." },
    { title: "VMs, then containers", tone: "cyan", icon: <Container />, text: "Many small workloads packed onto shared hardware, moving around all the time. More things on the network, talking to each other." },
    { title: "Microservices", tone: "blue", icon: <Boxes />, text: "Apps split into dozens or hundreds of small services that call each other over the network. One click can trigger a whole chain of internal calls." },
  ]}
/>

<Callout kind="insight">

Most traffic stopped crossing the edge at all. Cisco's Global Cloud Index forecast that more than 70% of data-centre traffic would stay *inside* the data centre.

</Callout>

### The network changed shape

- The classic data-centre network was a **tree**. Servers plugged into **access** switches, which fed **aggregation** switches, which fed one big **core** switch at the top, connected to the outside world. Great for traffic going up and out.
- For east-west traffic it's a detour: two servers in different branches talk all the way up through the core and back down, and every such call shares that one core.
- So data centres moved to **leaf-spine**: every **leaf** switch (servers plug into these) connects to every **spine** switch. Any two servers on different leaves are the same short distance apart, over many equal paths.

<Figure caption="Pick where server A sends its traffic. In the tree, far-apart servers climb to the single core. In leaf-spine, every cross-leaf path is the same length, and traffic can split over both spines.">
  <NetworkShapes />
</Figure>

<Callout kind="note">

The network literally changed shape because traffic changed direction.

</Callout>

## From castle-and-moat to zero trust

Security had to change too.

- **Google** was hit by a sophisticated attack in 2009, Operation Aurora. It then started **BeyondCorp**, described publicly in 2014: access depends on *who* the user is and *what device* they're on, not on being on the office network.
- That idea, now called zero trust, spread to service-to-service traffic too. Google's own services authenticate each other by identity (case file #3).

## East-west: the corridors

East-west traffic is internal, so teams used to assume it was safe. But there's a lot of it, and every call can fail. Every service that calls another needs answers to the same five questions:

### The sidecar: take the network out of the app

- The service makes a plain, simple call to `localhost`.
- Its sidecar handles discovery, load balancing, retries, timeouts, encryption and metrics, then forwards the call to the other service's sidecar, which hands it to the destination.
- Lyft built a proxy called **Envoy** for exactly this and open-sourced it in 2016 (case file #1). It became the building block of most service meshes.
- In 2017, Google, IBM and Lyft announced **Istio**, a control plane for Envoy proxies: it tells every sidecar the routing rules, security policies and certificates to use.

## Zero trust between services

With a sidecar on every service, every east-west call can be encrypted and authenticated with **mTLS**.

- In normal HTTPS, only the server proves who it is.
- In mTLS, *both sides* show a certificate. The payments service knows the caller really is the orders service, and the other way round.
- On top of that identity, you write rules:

```yaml
# file: payments-db-policy.yaml
# Only the payments service may talk to the payments database.
apiVersion: security.istio.io/v1
kind: AuthorizationPolicy
metadata:
  name: payments-db-access
spec:
  selector:
    matchLabels: { app: payments-db }
  rules:
    - from:
        - source: { principals: ["cluster.local/ns/prod/sa/payments"] }
```

<Callout kind="insight">

Now the corridors have locks, and every lock checks the badge. If an attacker takes over the recommendations service, they can't just walk over to the payments database: the proxy in front of it refuses any caller that isn't Payments.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="tip" title="When the alternative is the better choice">

- **A handful of services in one language?** A good library, or just sensible timeouts and TLS, is plenty.
- **Netflix** ran one of the world's largest microservice systems for years on in-process Java libraries, because most of its services were in Java. When it later adopted Envoy, it published how it made the move with almost no per-service configuration (case file #2). The right tool depended on its language mix and scale at the time.
- **North-south** traffic still belongs to a gateway, as Razorpay's story shows (case file #5).

</Callout>
