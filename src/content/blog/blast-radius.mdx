## In one minute

- Failures are guaranteed. **Blast radius** is how much one failure is allowed to damage: one request, one server, a slice of customers, or everyone.
- Shrink it in *space* with walls: **bulkheads** (a capped pool per dependency), **circuit breakers** (stop calling a dead service), **cells** (full copies of your stack, each serving a slice of customers) and **shuffle sharding**.
- Shrink it in *time* with **staged rollouts**: ship to 1% first, watch, then widen. In July 2024 one CrowdStrike update reached about 8.5 million Windows devices at roughly the same time.
- Walls only work if they are complete. One shared database or config file quietly undoes them, so find what everything shares, and test your walls on purpose.

## The Friday the screens went blue

- On **Friday, 19 July 2024**, airline check-in counters, hospital systems, bank branches and TV stations around the world showed the Windows "blue screen of death". Machines crashed, rebooted and crashed again.
- It wasn't a cyberattack. The security company CrowdStrike pushed a routine content update to its **Falcon sensor**, software that runs deep inside Windows to spot threats. The sensor couldn't handle the update, and crashed the whole operating system with it.
- Microsoft estimated about **8.5 million Windows devices** were affected, which it noted was less than one percent of all Windows machines. Less than one percent, and it still grounded flights.
- The bug was small. What made it global was its **reach**: the update went to everyone running that sensor at roughly the same time.

<Callout kind="important">

The question is not "will it break?" (it will). It is "**when it breaks, how much breaks with it?**"

</Callout>

## What blast radius means

The word comes from explosives, and the picture is the same: the bang will happen somewhere; what you decide is how far the damage spreads.

<Figure caption="The same bug, five possible fates. Good architecture doesn't make the bug disappear; it decides which ring the bug may reach. Hover or tap a fate to move the wall.">
  <RadiusRingsDiagram />
</Figure>

- Blast radius is mostly **not** about preventing failures. Disks die, deploys go wrong, people fat-finger commands.
- It is about deciding, **ahead of time**, how much each of those failures is allowed to hurt.
- Each failure domain is one blast radius. A single point of failure is a blast radius the size of your whole system.

<Analogy

    ["the toaster that shorts", "a bad deploy or a broken dependency"],

>
  Your toaster shorts out. There's a pop, the kitchen lights go off, and… that's it. The bedroom fan keeps spinning and the fridge in the hallway keeps humming. You walk to the fuse box, see one breaker flipped down, unplug the toaster and flip it back.

  That only works because the wiring is split into separate circuits, each with its own breaker. On one circuit, a faulty toaster would plunge every room into darkness. And if the main cable from the street fails, every circuit goes out anyway: separate circuits don't help against a failure in the one thing they all share.
</Analogy>

## Where the idea came from

The oldest version of this idea floats.

- Ships are split into **watertight compartments** by walls called bulkheads. Puncture the hull and water fills one compartment, the walls hold, and the ship stays afloat.
- According to the British inquiry, the **Titanic** could have stayed afloat with its four forward compartments flooded. The iceberg opened more than that, including a boiler room further back.
- Its bulkheads also didn't reach the top deck. As the bow sank lower, water poured over the top of one bulkhead into the next compartment, then the next.

<Figure caption="A generic ship, not a scale drawing of the Titanic. The bow is holed. With walls that stop below the deck, water spills over each wall top in turn; with walls that reach the deck, the flood stays where it came in. Switch the walls to compare.">

</Figure>

- So the steps below go from the smallest wall (one dependency) to the biggest (your whole fleet, over time).

## How to shrink the blast radius

### Step 1: Find what everything shares

Before adding walls, list your failure domains. For every part, ask: *if this broke right now, who would notice?* The scary answers are rarely things anyone calls "a service".

### Step 2: Bulkheads, so one slow thing can't eat everything

- A web server has **100 threads**. It calls two services: payments and recommendations.
- One day recommendations gets slow: **30 seconds** per request. Every thread that calls it gets stuck waiting.
- Soon all 100 threads are stuck on recommendations, and **nobody can pay**, even though payments is perfectly healthy.
- A **bulkhead** gives each dependency its own capped pool. Recommendations can tie up its own threads and no more.

<DemoFrame
  title="Drown the thread pool"
  hint="make it slow, then add bulkheads"
  caption="Each square is one thread. Calls stream in and hold a thread until they finish. Slow recommendations never finish, so with one shared pool they slowly take every thread. With bulkheads, recommendations gets 10 threads, payments 40, everything else 50."
>
  <BulkheadPoolDemo />
</DemoFrame>

```ts
// file: bulkhead.ts
// Each dependency gets its own small "compartment" of concurrent calls.
const recommendations = new Bulkhead({ maxConcurrent: 10 });
const payments = new Bulkhead({ maxConcurrent: 40 });

async function productPage(userId: string) {
  // If 10 recommendation calls are already stuck, fail fast instead of waiting.
  const recs = await recommendations.run(() => fetchRecs(userId)).catch(() => []);
  return render({ recs });
}
```

- A slow recommendations service can now tie up **at most 10 threads**.
- The page shows no recommendations; payments keep working. The flood stays in its compartment.

### Step 3: Circuit breakers, so you stop hammering a dead thing

Bulkheads cap the damage. **Circuit breakers** cut it off early.

- A breaker sits in front of a dependency and watches the results.
- **Closed:** calls go through as normal.
- **Open:** after too many failures it trips, and calls fail fast, without even trying, for a cool-down period.
- **Half-open:** after the cool-down it lets one test call through. If it works, the breaker closes. If not, it opens again.

<DemoFrame

  hint="break the service, then fix it"
  caption="Green calls go through and come back. Red calls are lost at the broken service. Amber calls are refused at once by the open breaker. Blue is the single test call while half-open."
>
  <CircuitBreakerDiagram />
</DemoFrame>

<Callout kind="tip">

Failing fast helps both sides. **You** don't pile up stuck threads, and the **dependency** isn't flooded with retries while it's trying to recover.

</Callout>

### Step 4: Cells, so a failure only reaches a slice of customers

Bulkheads and breakers protect you from *other* services failing. Your own service is the next risk: a bad deploy, a poison request or a corrupted cache can take down every copy of it, because every copy runs the same code with the same config.

- **Cell-based architecture** runs several complete, independent copies of your whole stack, called cells.
- Each cell has its own servers, its own database and its own queues, and serves only a slice of customers.
- A thin **router** in front sends each customer to their cell.
- With 10 cells, one cell failing hurts about **10%** of customers. With 100 cells, about **1%**.

### Step 5: Shuffle sharding, so one bad customer can't sink their neighbours

Cells have a weakness: everyone in a cell shares its fate. If one customer sends a poison request, everyone else in that cell suffers too.

- **Shuffle sharding** doesn't split customers into fixed groups.
- Instead, each customer gets its own **random combination** of a few workers.
- Two customers might share one worker, but it is very unlikely they share all of them.
- So when a poison customer crashes its workers, everybody else still has at least one healthy worker.

### Step 6: Shrink the blast radius in time

Everything so far shrinks blast radius in **space**: which customers a failure reaches. The CrowdStrike outage was about **time**: the bad update reached everyone before anyone could react.

- A **staged rollout** ships a change to a tiny slice first, maybe 1% of machines, watches health metrics, and only then widens to 10%, 50% and 100%.
- A **feature flag** goes further: new code ships "off", and you switch it on for a few users at a time, with a kill switch if it misbehaves.

```ts
// file: checkout.ts
if (flags.isEnabled("new-checkout", { userId, rolloutPercent: 5 })) {
  return newCheckout(cart); // 5% of users see this
}
return oldCheckout(cart); // flip the flag off and everyone is back here instantly
```

### Step 7: Test your walls on purpose

- Walls you've never tested are walls you're **hoping** work.
- **Chaos engineering** means injecting failures on purpose: killing servers, cutting network links, slowing dependencies.
- Do it in a controlled way, while people are watching, to prove your isolation holds before a real incident tests it for you.
- Netflix made this famous with Chaos Monkey (case file #5).

## Other ways to deal with failure

Isolation isn't the only strategy. Each alternative has its own everyday picture:

<Callout kind="real-world" title="Four everyday pictures">

- **The unsinkable hull.** Instead of compartments, build one hull so thick nothing could ever pierce it.
- **A spare tyre in the boot.** If one tyre goes flat, swap it and keep driving.
- **Two branches of the same restaurant.** Identical kitchens in two cities. If one floods, the other keeps serving; some customers just drive a bit further.
- **Nobody touch anything during the wedding.** On the big day nobody rewires the hall or tests the fire alarm. Change is risk, so you stop changing things.

</Callout>

<Quiz
  question="Your app runs on two identical servers behind a load balancer. A poison request crashes server 1, and the client retries. What happens?"
  options={["Server 2 handles it fine", "Server 2 crashes too: same code, same bug", "The load balancer blocks the request", "Server 1 restarts in time to handle it"]}

Redundancy handles accidents, like a dead disk. It does nothing against a failure that hits every copy the same way. In the cells demo, this is why more replicas wouldn't help: the shared database would still take everyone down.

<Callout kind="insight">

**Redundancy** is protection from accidents. **Isolation** is protection from yourself: the bad deploy you'll ship next month.

</Callout>

### When the simpler option wins

- For a small product with a few thousand users, a well-run single system with good backups, staged deploys and circuit breakers is usually the right call.
- Cells pay off at scale, when "everyone is down" costs real money every minute.

<Callout kind="real-world" title="Netflix chose the coarser wall">

Instead of slicing its service into many cells, Netflix invested in **active-active multi-region**: running in multiple AWS regions, so if one region has trouble, traffic can be evacuated to the others. Its 2013 engineering post describes the project. For a streaming service where any region can serve any member, "move everyone to a healthy region" is simpler than managing hundreds of cells, and it handles the scariest failure (a whole region going bad) directly.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="important">

The lesson isn't "always use cells". It's: **know your failure domains, and choose walls that match the failures you're most afraid of.**

</Callout>
